## Why the questions matter more than the quote

A quote tells you what a run costs when everything goes right. The agreement decides what happens when something goes wrong: a price jump, a rejected lot, a recall, or a plant that suddenly has no room for you. Ask these questions before you sign, get the answers in writing, and have a lawyer who works with food companies review the final agreement. University extension guides give the same advice: have your attorney, and possibly your accountant, review the contract before you sign ([UF/IFAS](https://edis.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/fs380), [Oklahoma State](https://extension.okstate.edu/fact-sheets/food-processing-using-a-co-packer)). This guide is a checklist, not legal advice.

## Who owns the formula?

This is the question founders most often skip, and the one that hurts most later.

- **If you bring the recipe,** the agreement should say you own it, along with any changes the plant makes while scaling it up.
- **If the plant develops the recipe,** ownership is whatever the contract says. Some plants keep ownership of formulas they create, or license them to you only while you manufacture there. If you want the right to take the product elsewhere, write that in before formulation starts.
- **Ask for the full formula and process in writing:** ingredient percentages, ingredient specifications, mixing and baking or drying parameters, and packaging specs. An ingredient list is not enough to make the product at another plant.

Scale-up changes recipes. Ask the plant to tell you, in writing, about any ingredient substitution before it happens. NC State Extension notes that substitutions can make your ingredient statement or nutrition information inaccurate ([NC State](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/choosing-and-using-a-copacker)). For a pet treat, that can mean a guaranteed analysis that no longer matches the product.

## What does an NDA protect, and what doesn't it?

Sign a mutual nondisclosure agreement before you send a formula or a spec. Oklahoma State Extension advises having one in place before you share your product spec or formulation ([Oklahoma State](https://extension.okstate.edu/fact-sheets/food-processing-using-a-co-packer)). Mutual matters because the plant will share its pricing and processes with you too.

Know the limits:

- **Scope can be narrow.** Some co-packers limit confidentiality agreements to written specifications, formulations, pricing, and customer information ([NC State](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/choosing-and-using-a-copacker)). Mark what you send as confidential, and keep a record of what you sent and when.
- **Confidentiality is never guaranteed.** The plant will see your formula, your volumes, and sometimes your customers, and NC State points out that agreements protect this only to a degree. It also notes that most co-packers will not agree to a noncompete, because it would restrict their business.
- **Trade secret protection depends on you.** Under US law, a recipe can be a trade secret only if you make reasonable efforts to keep it secret, and it can be lost through independent development or honest reverse engineering ([USPTO](https://www.uspto.gov/ip-policy/trade-secret-policy)). An NDA is one of those efforts, not the whole of them.

## What should the supply agreement say?

Most of the risk sits in the manufacturing or supply agreement. UF/IFAS lists the basics: the parties, the quantity, the quality and specifications, the production timeline, and the price, including payment method and due date ([UF/IFAS](https://edis.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/fs380)). Go further than that on each of these points.

**Pricing and price changes.** What is fixed, for how long, and what triggers a change? Ask whether the price can be held for a year, and what notice and documentation you get before an increase. UF/IFAS suggests asking for a guaranteed price for a year and checking for hidden fees.

**Minimums, forecasts, and purchase orders.** What is the minimum per run and per SKU? Is your forecast a commitment or an estimate? When does a purchase order become firm, and what happens if you cancel or change it? For how minimums work, see [co-packer minimums, lead times, and costs](/guides/co-packer-minimums-lead-times-and-costs/).

**Lead times.** From firm purchase order to ship, on first runs and repeats, and what happens if the plant misses a date.

**Quality specs and tolerances.** Weight, size, color, moisture, breakage, and any lab results, each with a target and an acceptable range. Specs that are not written down cannot be enforced.

**Acceptance and rejection.** How long you have to inspect a lot, how you reject it, who pays for rework or disposal, and how disputes get settled, for example by testing retained samples at an agreed lab.

**Retained samples and records.** How many units from each lot the plant keeps, for how long, and whether you can get batch records, certificates of analysis, and lot codes on request.

**Recalls.** A plant making a food whose hazards call for a preventive control must have a written recall plan, for human food ([21 CFR 117.139](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-117/subpart-C/section-117.139)) and for animal food, pet treats included ([21 CFR 507.38](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-E/part-507/subpart-C/section-507.38)). NC State suggests asking for a copy of the plant's plan. The agreement should say who decides on a recall, who notifies whom, and who pays when the cause is the plant's error versus your formula or label.

**Insurance and indemnity.** Ask whether you are covered under the plant's product liability insurance, and expect to carry your own to sell to stores ([NC State](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/choosing-and-using-a-copacker)). Ask for a certificate of insurance naming your company, and have your broker and lawyer check that the indemnity language and the coverage line up.

**Audits and certificates.** Your right to visit and audit the plant, and its duty to send current food safety certificates and audit results and to tell you about regulatory inspections that affect your product.

**Exclusivity.** A clause making the plant your only supplier, or requiring you to buy all of a product from it, can block a second plant later. Read about [adding a second source](/guides/second-source-co-packer-strategy/) before you agree to one.

**Term and termination.** How long the agreement runs, whether it renews automatically, how much notice either side owes to end it, and what counts as cause.

**Transition help.** Whether the plant will share process data and keep producing during a move, and on what terms.

**Packaging, ingredients, and buy-back.** Who owns printed film, cartons, printing plates, and cutters you paid for, and whether you must buy back leftover ingredients and packaging when you change a label, drop a SKU, or leave.

**Payment terms.** Deposits, net days, early payment discounts, and what happens to your product if an invoice is late.

## Questions to ask on the last call

1. If you develop or change my formula, who owns it, and will you give me the full formula and process in writing?
2. Will you sign a mutual NDA before I send a spec?
3. How and when can my price change?
4. What specs will you test each lot against, and how do I reject a lot?
5. Will you send your recall plan and certificate of insurance?
6. Is anything in this agreement exclusive?
7. If I leave, what do I owe for packaging and ingredients, and will you help with the transition?

A plant that answers these plainly, and puts the answers in the agreement, is usually one you can work with when a run goes wrong.
