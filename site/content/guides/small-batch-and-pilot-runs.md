## Why do co-packers have minimums?

Every time a line switches products, the plant cleans it, sets it up for the new product, and runs until the first good unit comes off. That labor and material costs roughly the same whether the run is small or large. The minimum is the run size at which your order pays for that changeover and still leaves the plant a margin.

So the way to a smaller first run is rarely to argue the number down. It is to make your changeover cheaper, or to pay for it directly. For how minimums, lead times, and prices fit together, see [co-packer minimums, lead times, and costs](/guides/co-packer-minimums-lead-times-and-costs/).

## What is a pilot run?

A pilot run, also called a test run or line trial, is a short production run of your product on the plant's real equipment, before you commit to a full order. It is not a sample made in a test kitchen. It answers a different question: does this formula work at production scale, on this line, with this packaging?

A good pilot run gives you:

- **Proof the formula scales.** Dough that behaves in a stand mixer can behave very differently in a commercial mixer (more on this below).
- **Shelf-life samples.** Product from the real line, in the real package, is what a shelf-life study needs.
- **Retailer and distributor samples.** Buyers want to see what they will actually receive, not a kitchen version.
- **Packaging trials.** You find out whether your bag seals, your carton fits the case, and your label goes on straight before you order packaging in volume.
- **Real numbers.** Yield, waste, and run speed, which feed the price for the full run.

## How do brands get a smaller first run?

Most of these work by making the run easier for the plant to fit in.

- **Use stock or simple packaging.** A stock pouch with a printed label changes over faster and costs less up front than custom printed film. Move to custom packaging once the product sells.
- **Keep labels simple.** A label applied to a stock package avoids long lead times on printed material.
- **Be flexible on timing.** If the plant can slot you into an open shift, it gains more from you than from a run it has to schedule around.
- **Start with fewer SKUs.** One or two flavors run cleanly before six do. Some plants let you split a minimum across SKUs: one pet treat bakery publishes a 10,000-pound minimum per recipe that can be split into two SKUs of 5,000 pounds each ([The Pound Bakery](https://www.thepoundbakery.com/pages/frequently-asked-questions)).
- **Pay for the line time instead.** Some co-packers price by the day rather than by a minimum. One small co-packer describes doing "a day's worth of work" in place of a unit minimum, and notes that a very small run costs more per unit, so brands should make as much as they can in that day ([Foodboro interview with Little Bird Kitchen](https://www.foodboro.com/ask-an-expert-co-packer/)).
- **Pay a setup or pilot fee.** Paying for the changeover directly can buy you a run below the usual minimum.

## Bridges before a co-packer

If even a small co-packer run is too large, a shared facility can carry you until volume catches up.

**Shared-use kitchens and incubators** lease commercial kitchen space and equipment to food businesses, often by the hour, and let you get the license needed to make products for sale elsewhere ([K-State Research and Extension](https://extension.k-state.edu/kansaslocalfoods/resources/process-food/shared-kitchens.html)).

**University food centers** go a step further in some states. Michigan State University's Food Processing and Innovation Center, for example, rents its facility to companies producing for test marketing or at small to mid-sized volumes ([MSU](https://www.canr.msu.edu/fpic/)).

Making pet treats? States regulate pet food, treats included, through product registration, a company license, or both, and most require it before you sell ([AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/resources/startups/registration-licensing/)). Confirm that the kitchen allows pet products and that your state's requirements are met.

## What does a pilot run cost?

Figures vary too much by product and plant to quote a meaningful number, but the structure is fairly consistent. Expect some mix of:

- **Setup and changeover labor,** billed as a fee or built into a day rate.
- **Line time,** by the hour, shift, or day.
- **Ingredients and packaging,** often at a higher unit cost because you are buying small quantities.
- **Formulation or R&D time,** if the plant adjusted your recipe for its equipment.
- **Testing,** such as shelf-life work, often through an outside lab.

Two terms matter most. First, whether the pilot fee is credited against your first production order. One co-packer describes crediting test-run money toward production as the general practice ([Foodboro](https://www.foodboro.com/ask-an-expert-co-packer/)), but ask rather than assume. Second, whether any of the output is sellable. The same co-packer notes it does not guarantee any usable product from a test run, because it is a test.

## Why a bench batch is not a production batch

The recipe that works in your kitchen is a starting point, not a production formula.

**Weights, not cups.** Home recipes use volume measures, which are too inconsistent for commercial production. Extension food specialists advise converting the recipe to weights, the most consistent measure, and then into a repeatable production formula ([University of Maryland Extension](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/product-development-part-2-scaling)).

**Equipment changes the product.** Ingredients behave differently in large commercial mixers than in small ones, and dwell times, temperature uniformity, and throughput on production equipment differ from the lab ([Baking Business](https://www.bakingbusiness.com/articles/65712-five-musts-when-scaling-from-bench-to-plant)). Mixing, spreading, bake color, and moisture can all shift.

**Plan for more than one try.** The same article reports bakery scale-up specialists recommending at least three test runs to work out production problems, and documenting every step of each run. Budget time and money for adjustment, not just one run.

## Shelf-life samples need a locked formula

Shelf-life data only means something for the final product in the final package. Oregon State's Food Innovation Center designs its shelf-life studies for products with a locked formula and final packaging, and offers both real-time storage and accelerated testing at elevated temperature ([Oregon State FIC](https://fic.oregonstate.edu/fic/quality-analysis-shelf-life/shelf-life-testing)). There is no single standardized method: labs tailor each study to the product, including its water activity, pH, and storage conditions ([MSU Extension](https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/understanding-shelf-life-testing-for-packaged-food-products)).

That is one more reason to run the pilot on production equipment. Kitchen samples tell you little about how the plant's product will hold up.

## How to read a small-batch offer

When a plant offers a run below its usual minimum, read the offer line by line.

1. **What is the real per-unit price at the small size, and at the next size up?** The gap shows what the small run costs you.
2. **Is there a setup, pilot, or day-rate fee, and is it credited to a later order?**
3. **Who buys the ingredients and packaging,** and are you committing to leftover packaging stock?
4. **Is the output sellable, sample-only, or unguaranteed?**
5. **Does the price assume stock packaging?** If you switch to custom film later, what changes?
6. **What does the next run look like?** A small first run is only useful if the reorder is realistic in size, price, and timing.
7. **Who owns formula changes the plant makes during scale-up?**

A small first run is a test for both sides. If you are moving from market stalls to retail, the steps before this one are in [from farmers market to packaged product](/guides/from-farmers-market-to-packaged-product/).
