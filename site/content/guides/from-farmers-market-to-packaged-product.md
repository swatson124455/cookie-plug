## What do cottage food laws let you do?

Cottage food laws are state laws that let you make certain foods in a home kitchen and sell them without a commercial food license, though some ask for a simpler registration instead. They vary widely by state, but many share the same limits ([Harvard Food Law and Policy Clinic](https://chlpi.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/12/FLPC_Cottage-Foods-Report_August-2018.pdf)):

- You sell directly to the consumer, not to stores, restaurants, or distributors.
- Annual sales are capped.
- Only foods that do not need refrigeration for safety qualify.
- Sales across state lines are not allowed.

Michigan's rules show the pattern. Cottage food must be sold directly to the consumer, consignment and wholesale are prohibited, gross sales are capped, and the law covers only foods that do not need time or temperature control for safety, such as breads, cookies, and cakes ([MDARD](https://www.michigan.gov/mdard/food-dairy/cottage-food/selling-and-samples), [MDARD FAQ](https://www.michigan.gov/mdard/food-dairy/cottage-food/getting-started-faq)). Your state may differ on every point, and these laws change, so read your state's current rules from its department of agriculture or health.

## Why does selling to stores usually need a licensed facility?

Whether you started at a market stand or a food truck, the rules change when a store resells your product. You are now making food for someone else to sell, and most cottage food laws stop at that line.

The reason is inspection. Michigan spells it out: a home kitchen is not licensed or inspected, so its food is not considered an approved source for a restaurant or grocery store, and even a baker renting a licensed kitchen needs a food establishment license of their own to sell to stores ([MDARD](https://www.michigan.gov/mdard/food-dairy/cottage-food)). Other states differ in the details, so ask your state's food licensing office. There is a federal layer too: facilities that manufacture, process, pack, or hold food must register with FDA. A private home is not a facility for this purpose, and a business whose primary function is selling food directly to consumers is exempt ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-industry/how-start-food-business), [FDA registration](https://www.fda.gov/food/guidance-regulation-food-and-dietary-supplements/registration-food-facilities-and-other-submissions)). A plant that makes your product for stores has to be registered.

Retail buyers then add their own requirements, starting with an audited food safety certificate for the plant (see [what big retailers require from a new supplier](/guides/what-retailers-require-from-a-new-supplier/)).

## Step 1: Lock the recipe by weight

Market recipes are often written in cups and "bake until golden." A manufacturer needs weights. Write every ingredient in grams or as a percent of the batch, with the brand and item of each ingredient, the bake time and temperature, the finished unit weight, and a photo of the product when it is right. Then make a batch from the written sheet alone. If it comes out the same, the sheet works.

## Step 2: Decide the shelf-life target

A cookie sold at Saturday's market only has to last the weekend. A packaged cookie has to survive the warehouse, the truck, the store shelf, and the customer's pantry. Decide the shelf life you need before you finalize the recipe and the package, because it drives both, and plan to test it. [Refrigerated to shelf-stable](/guides/refrigerated-to-shelf-stable/) covers water activity, preservatives, packaging, and shelf-life testing.

## Step 3: Packaging and a compliant label

FDA's [Food Labeling Guide](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/guidance-industry-food-labeling-guide) summarizes what a packaged food label must carry. The statement of identity and the net quantity go on the front (principal display) panel; the rest usually goes on the information panel.

| Element | What it says |
|---|---|
| Statement of identity | The common name of the food |
| Net quantity | The amount, in both metric and US units |
| Ingredient list | Every ingredient, in descending order of predominance by weight |
| Allergen declaration | Any major food allergen, named by its source |
| Name and place of business | Who made, packed, or distributed the food |
| Nutrition Facts | Calories and nutrients per serving |

The major allergens are milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and [sesame](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-allergies/faster-act-sesame-ninth-major-food-allergen). Name the source in parentheses after the ingredient, as in "flour (wheat)," or in a "Contains" statement next to the ingredient list ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/have-food-allergies-read-label)). If someone else makes your product, your company name must be qualified with a phrase such as "Manufactured for" or "Distributed by" ([21 CFR 101.5](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-A/section-101.5)).

Small companies may not need Nutrition Facts. FDA's [small business exemption](https://www.fda.gov/food/labeling-nutrition-guidance-documents-regulatory-information/small-business-nutrition-labeling-exemption) covers a product when the company has fewer than 100 full-time equivalent employees and sells fewer than 100,000 units of that product a year in the US. You must file a notice with FDA every year, unless you have fewer than 10 full-time equivalent employees and sell fewer than 10,000 units, and any nutrition claim on the label cancels the exemption. The exemption is FDA's, not your buyer's, so ask whether the retailer wants a panel before you print.

## Step 4: Get UPC barcodes from GS1 US

Stores scan a UPC barcode that carries your product's GTIN. Get yours from [GS1 US](https://www.gs1us.org/upcs-barcodes-prefixes/how-to-get-a-upc-barcode), where the barcode identifies your company as the brand owner. Individual GTINs suit a brand with a few products; a GS1 Company Prefix suits a growing line and also lets you create codes for cases and pallets. Each flavor and each size is a product a shopper is expected to tell apart, so each gets its own GTIN ([GS1](https://www.gs1.org/1/gtinrules/en/guiding-principles)).

## Step 5: Pick a co-packer or a shared-use kitchen

There are two usual routes into a licensed facility.

- **A shared-use kitchen.** You rent time in a licensed commercial kitchen and make the product yourself. You keep control and learn production, but your own labor sets the ceiling. Check whether your state requires your business, not just the kitchen, to hold a license (Michigan does, as noted above).
- **A co-packer.** A manufacturer makes and packs the product to your spec. You trade some hands-on control for capacity, consistency, and, if the plant holds one, the audited certificate retail buyers ask for.

If you are already in a shared kitchen and unsure when to move, [when to leave the commissary](/guides/when-to-leave-the-commissary/) covers the signs.

## If you make pet treats

Cottage food laws usually cover human food only, so pet treats generally fall under feed rules instead. FDA treats them as animal food, which the law requires to be safe, produced under sanitary conditions, free of harmful substances, and truthfully labeled ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-foods-feeds/pet-food)). The states regulate them as commercial feed, and state rules typically require the product to be registered, the company licensed, or both, before you sell ([AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/resources/startups/registration-licensing/)).

That applies to home businesses too. Michigan's cottage food law covers only human food, and its agriculture department licenses all animal food and treat products sold in the state ([MDARD](https://www.michigan.gov/mdard/animals/feed-safety/pet-food-and-treats)). Minnesota is an exception with limits: registered cottage food producers may sell baked or dehydrated treats for dogs and cats, but may not wholesale them to retailers ([Minnesota Department of Agriculture](https://www.mda.state.mn.us/food-feed/cottage-food-law-guidance)). If you sell online and ship to buyers in another state, FDA notes that the product should be registered in that state as well.

For the label, FDA requires the product's identity, net quantity, the name and place of business, and ingredients listed by weight ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-foods-feeds/how-do-i-start-animal-food-business)). State rules, largely based on AAFCO's models, add a guaranteed analysis: minimum crude protein and crude fat, maximum crude fiber and moisture ([AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/consumers/understanding-pet-food/reading-labels/)). If you plan to call your treats "human grade," AAFCO's definition requires every ingredient and the finished product to be handled under human-food manufacturing rules (21 CFR 117) in a facility licensed to make human food ([AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/hg_DOC.pdf)).
