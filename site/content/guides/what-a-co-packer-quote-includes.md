## What a quote is made of

A co-packer usually reviews your formula and gives you a per-unit cost to make and package the product, built from ingredients, packaging, and production costs that cover the plant's labor and overhead ([PartnerSlate](https://partnerslate.com/learningcenter/co-packer-costs-how-much-does-it-cost-to-get-your-food-or-beverage-product-produced/)). That single number hides a dozen decisions. Two quotes for the same cookie or baked dog treat can differ by a lot and both be honest, because they assume different things.

Break every quote into the lines below before you compare it with another.

## Ingredients: who buys them?

Ask whether the plant buys ingredients or you do, at what price, and with what markup. Then ask:

- Which ingredients are priced from a contract and which from the spot market?
- What yield or waste allowance is built in? Dough scraps, broken pieces, and startup product all count.
- Who pays for specialty or branded ingredients with long lead times or large pack sizes, and who owns what is left over?

For a baked dog treat, a named protein or a functional ingredient can drive the cost more than flour does, so ask for the ingredient line broken out.

## Packaging materials

Film, pouches, cartons, labels, trays, and shipping cases. Ask who buys them, how many you must order at a time, and what happens to leftover printed packaging when you change the artwork.

Printing method matters for small runs. Digital printing carries no plate charge, so short runs stay affordable; flexographic printing puts cost up front in plates and setup, then gets cheaper per package as volume grows ([Glenroy](https://www.glenroy.com/blog/flexographic-printing-vs-digital-printing/)). If a quote includes plates, ask who owns them and whether they move with you if you change plants.

## Labor, line time, and changeover

The plant charges for the people and hours on the line, from mixing through case packing. Changeover is the time spent cleaning and resetting the line between products; for why it drives minimums, see [co-packer minimums, lead times, and costs](/guides/co-packer-minimums-lead-times-and-costs/).

Ask whether setup and changeover are built into the unit price or billed as a separate fee per run, and whether a run below a certain size gets a flat charge. An allergen changeover, such as moving from a peanut butter cookie to a nut-free one, can take longer than an ordinary one.

## One-time fees

- **Formulation and R&D.** Scaling a kitchen recipe or developing a new one. Ask whether the fee is credited against your first order, and who owns the result.
- **Samples and trial runs.** Bench samples, then a pilot run on the production line.
- **Plates, dies, and cutters** for custom packaging or shapes.

## Testing

Testing is easy to leave out of a quote and hard to skip later.

- **Nutrition labeling.** Human food labels can be built from lab analysis or from ingredient databases; FDA publishes guidance on developing and using databases for nutrition labeling ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/guidance-industry-guide-developing-and-using-data-bases-nutrition-labeling)). Ask which the plant uses and who pays.
- **Pet treat label values.** A dog treat label needs a guaranteed analysis and a calorie statement per kilogram and per treat, either calculated or determined by feeding tests ([AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/resources/startups/calorie-content/)). Budget for lab work to confirm the numbers.
- **Shelf life.** Real storage studies take time and money. Oregon State University's Food Innovation Center, for example, lists standard shelf-life studies at $2,500 to $7,500, with final pricing depending on the number of SKUs, the study length, and the analyses involved ([Oregon State FIC](https://fic.oregonstate.edu/fic/quality-analysis-shelf-life/shelf-life-testing)).
- **Microbiological and finished-product testing.** Ask what the plant tests each lot for as standard, what costs extra, and whether product is held until results come back.

## Case packing, pallets, storage, and freight

Ask how many units go in a case, how cases are stacked on a pallet, and whether labeling cases and pallets is included. Then ask about storage: how long finished goods, ingredients, and packaging can sit at the plant for free, and the monthly rate after that. Finally, freight: where product ships from, whether the price is at the plant's dock or delivered, and who books the truck.

## Payment terms

Deposits, net days, and whether you pay for ingredients and packaging up front. Ask for the payment method and due date in writing, whether the price can be held for a year, and whether any fees are not on the quote ([UF/IFAS](https://edis.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/fs380)).

## All-in or tolling?

In a turnkey (all-in) arrangement, the plant orders the ingredients and packaging, makes the product, and bills you for finished goods. In tolling, you buy the ingredients and packaging and send them to the plant, which charges for its machines and labor ([PartnerSlate Academy](https://academy.partnerslate.com/service-model-turnkey-vs-tolling-in-cpg-contract-manufacturing)).

A tolling quote looks cheaper because it leaves out what you are buying yourself. To compare it with an all-in quote, add your ingredient and packaging costs, inbound freight to the plant, the cash you tie up in inventory, and your time coordinating deliveries. PartnerSlate notes that tolling leaves the brand much more to coordinate: materials must arrive before production, and testing and distribution have to line up with production dates.

## How volume changes the unit price

Higher volumes typically bring lower per-unit prices ([PartnerSlate](https://partnerslate.com/learningcenter/co-packer-costs-how-much-does-it-cost-to-get-your-food-or-beverage-product-produced/)), because setup, changeover, and one-time costs spread over more units, and larger ingredient and packaging orders cost less per unit. Ask every plant for prices at two or three volumes, such as your first run and the volume you expect in a year, so you can see the curve rather than a single point.

## Red flags in a quote

- A unit price with no mention of setup, changeover, testing, or storage.
- No yield or waste assumption.
- Ingredient and packaging costs you cannot see, with no way to check them later.
- A price that is only valid for a few days, with no mechanism for future changes.
- A quote given before the plant has seen or made your product.
- A request that you pay for the plant's equipment to get started, without clear terms on who owns it.

## A checklist for comparing two quotes

| Line | Quote A | Quote B | Ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unit price at first-run volume | | | Is this all-in or tolling? |
| Unit price at one-year volume | | | What volume triggers the break? |
| Ingredients | | | Who buys, what yield is assumed? |
| Packaging | | | Order minimums, who owns leftovers? |
| Setup and changeover | | | In the price or billed per run? |
| Formulation and samples | | | Credited against the first order? |
| Plates, dies, cutters | | | Who owns them? |
| Testing | | | Which tests per lot, who pays? |
| Case packing and pallets | | | Included, and what configuration? |
| Storage | | | Free days, then what rate? |
| Freight | | | From the dock or delivered? |
| Payment terms | | | Deposit, net days, price hold? |

Fill in every cell before you choose. A quote that cannot fill one of them is not finished yet.
