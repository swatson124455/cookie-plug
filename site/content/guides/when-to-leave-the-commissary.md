## The five signals

1. **A buyer asked for your audit certificate.** Large retailers expect an independent food safety audit of the plant that makes your product, and many require certification to a GFSI-benchmarked standard such as SQF or BRCGS; Walmart, for example, requires an annual approved audit of every supplier site. Shared kitchens rarely hold one. See [what big retailers require from a new supplier](/guides/what-retailers-require-from-a-new-supplier/).
2. **You said no to an order.** A wholesale account, a subscription box, a second region. If demand exists and you could not make it, the kitchen is the constraint.
3. **Your founders are baking.** If the people who should be selling are on the line at 5 a.m., growth stops.
4. **Your unit cost is not falling.** Hand production scales with labor hours; a line does not. When volume doubles and your cost per unit barely moves, you are paying for the kitchen.
5. **You are changing the product.** Going from refrigerated to shelf-stable, or from a bakery item to a packaged snack, is a formulation project a co-packer does routinely and a kitchen does once. See [refrigerated to shelf-stable](/guides/refrigerated-to-shelf-stable/).

## What the move involves

- **Benchmark.** Send the co-packer your product and let it make a match. Taste the two side by side, ideally blind.
- **Spec.** Write down ingredients, weights, tolerances, packaging, and shelf life. The spec is what the plant makes to, every run.
- **First run.** Sized to cover the plant's changeover, with packaging ordered early.
- **Handover.** Keep the kitchen running until the first run ships and sells through cleanly.

Expect a few months from first call to first pallet at a plant with capacity, and longer at one without. Printed packaging is often the longest lead time.

## Keeping quality

The fear is real and the fix is not glamorous: a written spec, a retained sample from every run, a taste check you run yourself on every lot, and a co-packer that lets you visit. Brands that lose quality in the move usually skipped the spec.

## What it costs, and what it saves

You pay a per-unit price that is higher than your ingredient cost and, once labor, rent, and your own hours are counted honestly, often lower than your all-in kitchen cost. You get back the founders' time, access to the certifications buyers ask for, and the ability to say yes to the next order. That last one is the whole point. For how pricing works, see [co-packer minimums, lead times, and costs](/guides/co-packer-minimums-lead-times-and-costs/).

## A note on control

Some founders keep a small in-house kitchen for development and special runs and use a co-packer for volume. That hybrid is common and a good way to start: you learn the co-packer on one product before you move the rest.

## If you make pet treats

The same signals apply, with one more: pet treats are regulated as animal food, and states regulate their sale through product registration, company licensing, or both. A manufacturer that already makes pet products knows that paperwork. See [how to find a dog treat co-packer](/guides/how-to-find-a-dog-treat-co-packer/).
