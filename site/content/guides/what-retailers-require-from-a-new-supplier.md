## Why is GFSI certification the first gate?

Large grocery chains want proof that the plant making your product runs a food safety system an independent auditor has checked. The Global Food Safety Initiative (GFSI) does not certify plants itself. It benchmarks certification programs, and independent certification bodies audit plants against those programs and issue the certificates ([GFSI](https://mygfsi.com/how-to-implement/frequently-asked-questions-faq/)). GFSI-recognized programs include [SQF, BRCGS, FSSC 22000, and IFS](https://mygfsi.com/how-to-implement/recognition/certification-programme-owners/).

The details differ by retailer, by category, and between national brands and store brands, but the pattern holds: an independent audit of the plant, usually against a GFSI-recognized program. From their published requirements:

- **Walmart** requires an annual food safety audit of every site that makes or processes product it sells, regardless of who owns the site, and does not accept FDA, USDA, state, or local inspections in place of it ([Walmart](https://enablement.walmart.com/content/food-safety/en_us/food-safety-requirements/national-branded-products/all-other-commodities.html)). For some categories, pet treats among them, it requires GFSI-recognized certification specifically.
- **Costco** has its own addendum that auditors can run alongside a GFSI certification audit ([FSSC 22000](https://www.fssc.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/FSSC-22000-Costco-Requirements-Module-V3-12022024.pdf)).
- **Target** has required GFSI certification from the facilities that make its owned-brand foods since 2009 ([Target](https://corporate.target.com/sustainability-governance/operating-ethically/product-safety-quality-assurance/food-safety)).
- **Kroger** was among the first large retailers to require GFSI-recognized certification from suppliers ([GFSI case study](https://mygfsi.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/GFSI-Case-Study-Booklet-2020-Kroger.pdf)), and its Our Brands (store brand) policy requires it for each manufacturing plant ([Kroger](https://www.thekrogerco.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/OBA-Vendor-Certification-Policy-Aug-2019.pdf)).

Certification is not a one-time event. SQF, for example, recertifies sites every year, and at least once every three years that audit is unannounced ([SQFI](https://www.sqfi.com/integrity-program/select-site-program)). Walmart warns that failing to maintain an annual approved audit can cost a supplier its business and its vendor number.

## Whose certificate does the buyer check?

The plant's. These requirements attach to the site where the food is made: Walmart's to every manufacturing site, Kroger's to each plant, Target's to each facility making its owned-brand food. When a co-packer makes your product, the co-packer's certificate is the one that counts.

Ask for it early, before you pitch, and read it:

- The site name and address match the plant that will make your product.
- The scope covers your kind of product. The certification body sets the scope, and products outside it are not covered.
- It is current, and you know when the next audit is due.

For SQF, you can verify a certificate, and check for suspended or withdrawn ones, in SQFI's [public directory](https://www.sqfi.com/directories/sqf-assessment-database). If the plant is not certified yet, ask when its first audit is scheduled and plan your pitch around that date.

## What insurance will the retailer ask for?

Expect to provide a certificate of insurance (COI) that names the retailer as an additional insured. Whole Foods Market, for example, requires suppliers to name it as an additional insured and sets coverage by the risk level of the product ([Whole Foods Market](https://www.wholefoodsmarket.com/company-info/information-potential-suppliers), [insurance guidelines](http://assets.wholefoodsmarket.com/www/vendors/SoPac/WFM_Supplier_InsuranceRequirements_2_20_14.pdf)).

You are the vendor, so the certificate is yours, not the co-packer's. Ask your co-packer for its own COI naming your company, and ask your insurance broker what your policy would cover in a recall.

## What barcodes and product data do you need?

Each consumer unit needs a GTIN, carried in the UPC barcode that stores scan. Get them from [GS1 US](https://www.gs1us.org/upcs-barcodes-prefixes/how-to-get-a-upc-barcode); a GS1 barcode identifies your company as the brand owner, which retailers look for. A GS1 Company Prefix also lets you create case codes (GTIN-14) and serial shipping container codes (SSCCs, 18 digits) for pallets and other logistics units; the SSCC travels in a GS1-128 barcode on the [logistics label](https://www.gs1us.org/upcs-barcodes-prefixes/gs1-128).

Then expect item setup to ask for exact numbers: unit, case, and pallet dimensions and weights, units per case, cases per layer and layers per pallet, shelf life, and storage conditions. Most of these come from the packaging line, so get them from the plant in writing, measured on real production rather than a mockup.

## Is your label ready for a buyer?

The label has to meet FDA rules before any buyer's standards ([FDA Food Labeling Guide](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/guidance-industry-food-labeling-guide)):

- The product name and net quantity (in metric and US units) on the front panel.
- Ingredients in descending order of predominance by weight.
- The [major food allergens](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/food-allergies) declared by source: milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and sesame (required since January 1, 2023).
- The name and place of business, qualified with "Manufactured for" or "Distributed by" when someone else makes the product ([21 CFR 101.5](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-A/section-101.5)).
- Nutrition Facts, unless an exemption applies.

Then check the retailer's own standards. Whole Foods Market, for example, publishes a [list of ingredients it will not accept](https://www.wholefoodsmarket.com/quality-standards/food-ingredient-standards) in the food it sells.

## Are you ready for a recall?

FDA already requires most food businesses to keep records of where each food came from and where it went, one step back and one step forward ([21 CFR part 1, subpart J](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-1/subpart-J)). A facility making a food that needs a preventive control must also keep a written recall plan covering how it will notify direct customers and the public, check that the recall worked, and dispose of the product ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/files/guidance%20documents/published/Hazard-Analysis-and-Risk-Based-Preventive-Controls-for-Human-Food-Chapter14-Recall-Plan.pdf)). Retailers and certification schemes build on that: Target lists lot traceability, including open date codes, among its requirements for owned-brand food, and the [SQF code](https://www.sqfi.com/docs/sqfilibraries/code-documents/edition-9/code-pdfs/20227fmin_foodmanufacturing_v3-2-final-w-links.pdf?sfvrsn=7f70c75a_8) requires plants to test their recall system at least once a year.

Your part as the brand: link the plant's lot codes to your shipping records so you know which lots went to which customer, keep a current contact for every account, and run a mock recall with your co-packer before a buyer asks for one.

## Do you need EDI and a supplier portal?

Plan on both. Kroger, for example, tells new vendors they are [required to use EDI](https://edi.kroger.com/EDIPortal/FAQ.html), which generally means receiving purchase orders (the 850 transaction) and sending invoices (the 810) electronically ([Kroger EDI requirements](https://edi.kroger.com/EDIPortal/EDIGuideAndReq_Kroger.html)). Large retailers also run supplier portals; Walmart's is [Retail Link](https://supplier.walmart.com/). If a distributor sits between you and the retailer, it may handle EDI for you, but your item data and lot records still have to be right.

## What changes for pet retail?

FDA regulates pet food and treats as animal food, the states regulate them as commercial feed, and pet retailers add their own standards.

- Walmart requires GFSI-recognized certification for every site that makes the national-brand pet treats it sells, again regardless of ownership ([Walmart](https://enablement.walmart.com/content/food-safety/en_us/food-safety-requirements/national-branded-products/pet-treats.html)).
- Petco announced in 2018 that it would stop selling dog and cat food and treats containing artificial colors, flavors, and preservatives ([AVMA](https://www.avma.org/javma-news/2019-01-15/petco-moving-away-artificial-ingredients)). Check its current standards before you formulate.
- States regulate pet food sales through product registration, company licensing, or both ([AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/resources/startups/registration-licensing/)), so check each state you sell in. State label rules, largely based on AAFCO's models, call for a guaranteed analysis: minimum crude protein and crude fat, maximum crude fiber and moisture ([AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/consumers/understanding-pet-food/reading-labels/)).

## A checklist to work through with your co-packer

| Requirement | Whose it is | What to ask the plant for |
|---|---|---|
| GFSI-benchmarked certificate | The plant | The current certificate and its scope |
| Certificate of insurance | You, as the vendor | Its own COI naming your company |
| GTINs and case and pallet codes | You, as the brand owner | The case and pallet configuration |
| Item, case, and pallet data | You, using plant data | Measured dimensions and weights |
| Label compliance | You | Final formula and nutrition data |
| Traceability and recall | Both of you | Lot code format and a joint mock recall |
| EDI and portals | You or your distributor | Ship dates and lot numbers for each order |
