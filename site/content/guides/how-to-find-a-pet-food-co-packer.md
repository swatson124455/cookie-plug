## Start with your format

"Pet food co-packer" covers plants that share almost no equipment. Name your format before you search, because it decides which plants are even candidates.

- **Extruded kibble** is cooked under heat and pressure in an extruder, then dried and usually coated. Extrusion lines are built for volume, so ask about run sizes early.
- **Baked** foods and biscuits run on ovens much like human bakery lines, which is why baked formats often have the most co-packer options.
- **Freeze-dried** products need vacuum freeze dryers, a slow batch process, and often start from raw meat.
- **Air-dried** products are dried slowly at low temperature, with their own dryers and handling.
- **Wet or canned** food is sealed and heat-processed in a retort. Because it is a low-acid food in a hermetically sealed container, FDA's rules for thermally processed low-acid foods apply on top of the animal food rules ([21 CFR 500.23](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-E/part-500/subpart-B/section-500.23)), so only plants set up for that work can make it.
- **Raw** (frozen or freeze-dried) needs strict pathogen controls. FDA warns that raw pet food can carry *Salmonella* and *Listeria monocytogenes*, putting both pets and the people who handle the food at risk ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-health-literacy/get-facts-raw-pet-food-diets-can-be-dangerous-you-and-your-pet)). Expect a plant to ask hard questions about your safety plan, and ask it the same.

If your product is a treat rather than a meal, start with [how to find a dog treat co-packer](/guides/how-to-find-a-dog-treat-co-packer/).

## Complete and balanced, or intermittent feeding?

Decide this before you talk to a formulator, because it drives your recipe, your label, and your cost.

To say "complete and balanced," a dog or cat food must either meet one of AAFCO's Dog or Cat Food Nutrient Profiles or pass a feeding trial using AAFCO procedures ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-health-literacy/complete-and-balanced-pet-food)). A complete and balanced food is meant to be the animal's sole diet.

- **Formulation method.** The food is formulated to meet the nutrient profile, and the label says so. Keep in mind that the profiles are expressed on a dry matter basis, while the guaranteed analysis on the bag is as fed, so the numbers need converting before you compare them.
- **Feeding trial method.** Animals eat the food as their only source of nutrients under an AAFCO protocol. For adult maintenance, the protocol runs at least 26 weeks ([AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Pet_Food_Report_2013_Midyear-Proposed_Updates_to_Feeding_Protocols.pdf)). Trials take longer and cost more, so ask early whether you need one.

A food that meets neither standard is labeled for intermittent or supplemental feeding only. Products clearly labeled as snacks, treats, or supplements do not need a nutritional adequacy statement ([AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/eight_required_labeilng_items.pdf)).

## Which rules apply to the plant?

Pet food does not need FDA approval before it goes on sale, but it is still subject to FDA regulation ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/AnimalVeterinary/ResourcesforYou/ucm047107.htm)). For the plant, that means:

- **Food facility registration.** A plant that makes, processes, packs, or holds animal food in the US must register with FDA as a food facility unless an exemption applies.
- **21 CFR part 507.** Registered animal food facilities must follow current good manufacturing practice and, unless an exemption applies, run a food safety plan with a hazard analysis and risk-based preventive controls ([21 CFR 507](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-E/part-507)). Ask to see how the plant's plan covers your product.

States add their own layer. There is no federal product registration or licensing for pet food; each state has its own requirements, forms, and fees. Depending on the state, it registers product labels, licenses the company, or both ([AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/resources/startups/registration-licensing/)). Online sales count too: AAFCO explains that shipping product into a state to fill an internet order is distribution into that state ([AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/resources/startups/internet-farmers-market/)). AAFCO notes that some companies hire a registration agent to handle the filings. Plan the states you will sell in before you print packaging.

## What goes on the label?

AAFCO describes eight required items: the brand and product name, the species, and the net quantity on the front panel, plus an ingredient statement, a guaranteed analysis, a nutritional adequacy statement, feeding directions, and the name and address of the manufacturer or distributor, who is the guarantor of the product ([AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/eight_required_labeilng_items.pdf)). A few details founders miss:

- **Guaranteed analysis.** State label rules, largely based on AAFCO's models, call for minimum crude protein and crude fat and maximum crude fiber and moisture ([AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/consumers/understanding-pet-food/reading-labels/)).
- **Feeding directions.** Expected on foods labeled for a life stage. They are optional for products labeled as snacks or treats that are not complete and balanced.
- **Calorie content.** Stated as metabolizable energy on an as-fed basis, in kilocalories per kilogram and per familiar unit such as a cup, can, or piece, under its own "Calorie Content" heading, separate from the guaranteed analysis, and the label must say whether the figure was calculated or determined by feeding tests ([AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/resources/startups/calorie-content/)).

The format is changing. AAFCO's membership [approved new model pet food regulations](https://www.aafco.org/news/aafco-membership-approves-new-model-pet-food-and-specialty-pet-food-regulations/) in 2023 that add a Pet Nutrition Facts box and an intended use statement on the front panel; labels following the older model remain acceptable during a transition period ([AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/pflm/)). Ask your co-packer and label reviewer which format your states expect before you print.

## Where to look for a pet food co-packer

- **Co-manufacturing marketplaces.** [PartnerSlate](https://partnerslate.com/petfood/) runs a pet food section where you describe your product and get matched with plants.
- **Trade shows.** At [Petfood Forum](https://www.petfoodforumevents.com/about/petfood-forum-101), exhibitors include co-packers alongside ingredient, packaging, and equipment suppliers, which makes it a good place to meet several plants in two days.
- **Suppliers and consultants.** Ingredient and packaging reps, pet food formulators, and state registration agents work with many plants and know which ones take new brands.
- **Other founders.** Brands one step ahead of you know which plants answer and which ones quote and disappear.

## Questions to ask each plant

1. Which of my formats do you run today, and on what equipment?
2. Have you made complete and balanced foods, and which substantiation method did your customers use?
3. Will you walk me through how your food safety plan under part 507 covers my product?
4. Who handles state registrations and label review?
5. If you also run human food, how do you separate the two?
6. What is the minimum per SKU, and what happens on the first run?
7. Who owns the formula if you develop it? See [questions to ask before you sign](/guides/questions-to-ask-a-co-packer-before-signing/).

## Sample before you commit

Ask for a bench sample, then a pilot run on the production line, before you sign. Check it the way a customer would: palatability, kibble or piece size, breakage in the bag, and smell after opening. Send pilot product to a lab to confirm the guaranteed analysis, and plan shelf-life work before launch. If a retailer is next, read [what big retailers require from a new supplier](/guides/what-retailers-require-from-a-new-supplier/).
