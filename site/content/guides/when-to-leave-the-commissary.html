title: When to Leave the Commissary: Signs a Brand Has Outgrown Its Kitchen description: The five signals that a food or pet-treat brand should move from a shared kitchen to a co-packer, what the transition involves, and how to keep quality through it. date: 2026-10-01

Short answer: move when the kitchen, not demand, sets your ceiling: when a retail buyer asks for a certification the kitchen cannot provide, when a launch needs more units than your team can bake, when you are turning down wholesale, or when your hourly cost per unit is higher than a co-packer's quote. The transition takes one to three months and starts with a benchmark sample.

The five signals

  1. A buyer asked for your audit certificate. Sprouts, Whole Foods, and pet specialty chains increasingly require a GFSI-benchmarked facility (SQF or BRC). Shared kitchens rarely have one.
  2. You said no to an order. A wholesale account, a subscription box, a second region. If demand exists and you could not make it, the kitchen is the constraint.
  3. Your founders are baking. If the people who should be selling are on the line at 5am, growth stops.
  4. Your unit cost is not falling. Hand production scales linearly; a line does not.
  5. You are changing format. Going from refrigerated to shelf-stable, or from a bakery item to a packaged snack, is a formulation project a co-packer does daily and a kitchen does once.

What the transition involves

Benchmark first: send the co-packer your product and let them match it. Taste blind. Then the spec: ingredients, weights, tolerances, packaging, shelf life. Then a first run at a size that covers their changeover. Expect one to three months from first call to first pallet if the plant has capacity, longer if it does not.

Keeping quality

The fear is real and the fix is boring: a written spec, a retained sample from every run, a taste panel you run yourself on every lot, and a co-packer who lets you visit. Brands that lose quality in the move usually skipped the spec.

What it costs, and what it saves

You pay a per-unit price that is higher than your ingredient cost and lower than your all-in kitchen cost once labor and rent are counted honestly. You get back the founders' time, the certifications, and the ability to say yes to the next order. That last one is the whole point.

A note on control

Some founders keep a small in-house line for development and special runs, and use a co-packer for volume. That hybrid is common and it is a good way to start: you learn the co-packer on one SKU before moving the rest.
