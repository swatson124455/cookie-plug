title: Co-Packer Minimums, Lead Times, and What They Actually Cost description: How minimum order quantities, lead times, and tolling fees work at a contract food manufacturer, why minimums keep rising, and how to negotiate a first run. date: 2026-10-01

Short answer: a co-packer's minimum is the smallest run that covers a line changeover, so it rises when the plant is busy and falls when it has room. Lead times work the same way. If a plant is quoting you a huge minimum and a long wait, it is telling you it is full.

Why minimums exist

Every time a line switches products, the plant cleans it, re-sets it, and runs waste until the first good unit comes off. That costs hours of labor and ingredients whether you order 5,000 units or 50,000. The minimum is where your order covers that cost. That is why minimums differ by line: a wire-cut cookie line changes over faster than a sandwich line with cream deposit.

Why they keep going up

A busy plant would rather run one big customer for three days than three small ones. Raising minimums is how it politely says no to small brands. The opposite is also true: a plant with open capacity gains more from filling a shift than from holding out for a bigger order, so it can be flexible on a first run and grow the minimum with you.

What you actually pay

Three numbers, usually: a per-unit tolling or all-in price (the plant supplies ingredients and packaging, or you do), a one-time formulation or setup fee if they develop the recipe, and freight. Ask which ingredients and packaging are included in the unit price. Ask whether the formulation fee is credited against the first order. Ask for the price at two volumes so you can see the curve.

Lead times, realistically

From approved sample to first production run: weeks at a plant with room, months at a full one. From purchase order to ship on repeat runs: a few weeks is normal, and it depends on packaging lead times as much as production. Order your printed packaging before you need it; it is the most common cause of a late first run.

Negotiating a first run

Do not argue the minimum down; make the first run cheap for the plant instead. Use a stock pouch or a simple label so changeover is short. Accept a longer shelf-life window so they can run when the line is open. Offer a clean forecast for the next three runs. A plant will bend its minimum for a customer who looks like a good repeat.

The number that matters more than the minimum

Your reorder. A retailer's first order is a test; the second is the business. If your co-packer cannot run the reorder on time, the listing is gone. Ask every plant one question: "If my retailer doubles the order in month three, what happens?" The answer tells you whether they have capacity or a waitlist.
