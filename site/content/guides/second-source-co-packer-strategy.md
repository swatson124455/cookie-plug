## What can go wrong with one co-packer?

Most single-source problems are ordinary events that happen to land on your only plant. None of them is your fault, and each one can stop shipments.

- **A capacity squeeze.** The plant fills up with larger customers and your runs slide down the schedule. Minimums rise as a plant gets busier (see [co-packer minimums](/guides/co-packer-minimums-lead-times-and-costs/)).
- **A failed or lapsed audit.** Your retail listings may depend on the plant's certificate. Walmart, for example, warns that a supplier that fails to maintain an annual approved audit can lose the business and have its vendor number deactivated ([Walmart](https://enablement.walmart.com/content/food-safety/en_us/food-safety-requirements/national-branded-products/all-other-commodities.html)).
- **A recall.** Even a recall of another brand's product can tie up the plant's lines, people, and attention while it is investigated.
- **A fire, flood, or equipment failure.** One oven or one packaging machine can stop every SKU that runs on it.
- **Loss of interest.** Your runs stay small, the plant's priorities change, or it is sold and the new owner has different plans.

A second plant that has already made your product does not prevent any of this. It keeps product moving while you deal with it.

## When should you add a second source?

Before you need it. Qualifying a plant takes samples, trial runs, and paperwork, and none of that goes well during a stockout. Good moments to start: before a large retail launch, when one plant makes every SKU you sell, or when your primary starts quoting longer lead times, higher minimums, or missing ship dates.

It also helps to have the answer ready when someone asks. Continuity of supply is a fair question for a retail buyer or an investor, and "we have a second plant that already makes our product" is a strong answer.

## How do you qualify a second plant?

Use the same process you used for the first (see [how to find a cookie co-packer](/guides/how-to-find-a-cookie-co-packer/)), with a few extra checks:

1. **Line fit.** It runs your format and pack size today, without new equipment.
2. **Certificate.** It holds a GFSI-benchmarked certificate for that site, with a scope that covers your product. For SQF sites, you can confirm the certificate, and check for suspended or withdrawn ones, in SQFI's [public directory](https://www.sqfi.com/directories/sqf-assessment-database).
3. **FDA history.** FDA's [inspection classification database](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/inspection-classification-database) is searchable by firm name and shows whether each inspection ended with no action, voluntary action, or official action indicated. It does not include inspections done by states. FDA's [warning letters](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/compliance-actions-and-activities/warning-letters) are searchable by company too.
4. **Registration.** Ask for confirmation that its FDA food facility registration is current. Registrations must be renewed between October 1 and December 31 of each even-numbered year ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/online-registration-food-facilities/food-facility-registration-user-guide-biennial-registration-renewal)).
5. **Allergens.** Find out which allergens run on the lines you would use and how the plant keeps them apart.
6. **A benchmark.** Send your product and your spec, get the plant's version back, and taste the two blind.

## What should you move first?

One SKU, or a slice of one SKU's volume. Both limit the damage if the new plant stumbles.

- **One SKU.** Pick a stable, simple product with steady demand, not the seasonal item with the complicated inclusion. The second plant learns your standards on something forgiving.
- **A slice of volume.** Let the second plant supply one region, one retailer, or one distribution center while your primary keeps the rest. Both plants make the same product, so you learn quickly whether they match.

Run the second plant through several production cycles before you depend on it, and compare its lots with your primary's side by side each time. Move what you need for continuity, not everything you can.

## How do you keep two plants making the same product?

Two plants will match only if the spec leaves them nothing to guess. Write down:

- **The formula by weight,** with a tolerance for each ingredient and for finished unit weight.
- **Process targets:** bake time and temperature ranges, finished color, dimensions, and a moisture or water activity range.
- **Approved ingredients** by supplier and item number, with a rule that any substitution needs your written approval and a side-by-side test.
- **Packaging specs:** film, trays, cases, labels, case count, and the pallet pattern.
- **Lot coding** that includes a plant identifier, so you can tell where any package came from. You will need that link to trace a lot one step back and one step forward, as FDA's [recordkeeping rules](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-1/subpart-J) expect.
- **Retained samples** from every lot at both plants, kept through the end of shelf life, plus a taste check you run yourself.

Shared packaging can work. When your label names your company with "Manufactured for" or "Distributed by," FDA's labeling rule does not require the plant's name on the package ([21 CFR 101.5](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-A/section-101.5)), so both plants can run the same printed film. Before you rely on that, confirm that the Nutrition Facts and any allergen statements are true for product from both plants.

## How do you tell your primary co-packer?

Early, directly, and before the second plant runs your product. Frame it as what it is: ordinary risk management that any growing brand should have. Say plainly what stays with the primary (most of the volume, the SKUs it runs best, your forecast) and what you expect from them that will not change.

A primary that keeps most of your volume and gets a reliable forecast has less to lose than it may first think. Hearing the news from you, before the trial runs, keeps the conversation about planning instead of trust. If your primary developed your recipe, you may need its cooperation, which makes the contract the first thing to read.

## What should your contracts say?

Read your current agreement before you do anything else, and have a lawyer review any changes. Look for:

- **Formula ownership.** If the plant developed your recipe, the contract decides who owns it. If you want the right to make it elsewhere, get that right in writing, along with the full formula and process, not just the ingredient list.
- **Exclusivity.** A clause that makes the plant your sole supplier, or requires you to buy all of a product from it, can block a second source outright. Watch for minimum volume commitments with penalties too.
- **Notice periods.** How much notice each side owes to end the agreement, change prices, or discontinue a product.
- **Packaging, ingredients, and tooling.** Who owns printing plates, cutters, and molds you paid for, and whether you must buy back leftover packaging and ingredients if volume moves.
- **Transition help.** Whether the plant will share process data and keep running your product while a second plant ramps up.
- **Retailer approval.** If you also make a store brand for a retailer, check that retailer's rules. Kroger's Our Brands policy, for example, bars vendors from subcontracting manufacturing to another plant without prior notice and Kroger's written approval ([Kroger](https://www.thekrogerco.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/OBA-Vendor-Certification-Policy-Aug-2019.pdf)).

When you sign with the second plant, write the same points in from the start, so the next change is easier than this one.
