## What is the difference between private label and co-packing?

The difference is whose formula goes in the bag.

- **Private label** means a manufacturer makes a treat from a formula it already has, a stock recipe or one it owns, and packs it under your brand. You choose from its menu, add your packaging, and sell.
- **Co-packing**, also called contract manufacturing, means a manufacturer makes your formula to your specification. The recipe is yours, whether you bring it or pay to develop it.

Both put your brand name on the front of the bag. What sits behind that name, and who controls it, is where they part ways.

## What about "white label"?

Suppliers use these words loosely. Some say "white label" for a generic product sold identically to many brands and keep "private label" for something made only for you. Others use the two terms interchangeably. When a supplier offers "private label," ask two direct questions: is this formula sold to other brands, and can I change it? The answers matter more than the label on the offer.

## Private label vs co-packing at a glance

| Factor | Private label | Co-packing |
|---|---|---|
| Formula | The manufacturer's stock or owned recipe | Your recipe, brought or developed |
| Speed to market | Faster: the product already runs | Slower: sampling, scale-up, and approval come first |
| Minimums | Often smaller | Often larger, since the run is dedicated to you |
| Differentiation | Limited: others may sell the same treat | High: the product is yours alone |
| Formula ownership | Usually stays with the manufacturer | Yours, if the contract says so |
| Cost to start | Lower: little or no development | Higher: formulation, testing, first-run setup |
| Switching plants | Hard: the recipe may not come with you | Easier: you can take the formula to another plant |

## How do speed and minimums compare?

A private label treat already runs on the manufacturer's line, has a known shelf life, and has a working label. You mostly wait on your own packaging. A custom formula needs samples, adjustments, and a first production run before you have anything to sell.

Minimums can differ too, since the plant already knows how a stock recipe runs. One pet treat bakery publishes minimums of 2,000 to 4,000 pounds per item per run for private label and an average of 4,000 pounds per formula per run for contract manufacturing ([Foppers](https://www.foppers.com/privatelabelpettreatsbyfoppers)). Another publishes custom-recipe minimums starting at 10,000 pounds per recipe, which can be split across two SKUs ([The Pound Bakery](https://www.thepoundbakery.com/pages/frequently-asked-questions)). Those are single companies' published terms, not market rates, so ask every supplier for both numbers. For more on why minimums exist, see [co-packer minimums, lead times, and costs](/guides/co-packer-minimums-lead-times-and-costs/).

## Differentiation and formula ownership

With private label, a shopper can sometimes find the same treat under another brand at a lower price. Your packaging, story, and channel have to carry the difference. That can work, especially if you sell through your own audience, but it is a weaker position when a retail buyer compares you to shelf neighbors.

With co-packing, the formula can be a real point of difference: a single protein, a novel ingredient, a texture nobody else has. Ownership is not automatic, though. If the plant's R&D team developed the recipe, the contract decides who owns it. Get that in writing before development starts.

## How does cost compare?

Private label usually costs less to start: no formulation fee, fewer test batches, and existing specifications. Over time, though, you have less leverage on the per-unit price, because you cannot take the recipe to another plant for a competing quote.

Co-packing costs more up front: formulation, samples, and sometimes a setup or pilot run. As volume grows, you can negotiate the per-unit price on a product you control, change an ingredient, or quote the same formula at a second plant.

## Who handles the regulatory work?

This is the part founders overlook.

**The guarantor.** Every pet food label, treats included, names a company responsible for the product. Federal rules require the label to state the name and place of business of the manufacturer, packer, or distributor ([21 CFR 501.5](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/501.5)). AAFCO calls that company the guarantor: the party responsible for the product and its labeling ([AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/consumers/understanding-pet-food/reading-labels/)).

**"Manufactured for" labeling.** If the company named on the label did not make the product, federal rules require a qualifying phrase such as "Manufactured for" or "Distributed by" ([eCFR, Part 501](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-E/part-501)). With a co-packer, your brand usually appears this way, which makes you the guarantor.

**State registration.** States regulate pet food sales, treats included. Most require product registration, a license for the company, or both, before you sell there, and no single agency approves a product for every state ([AAFCO](https://www.aafco.org/resources/startups/registration-licensing/)). Some states tie the duty to the label: Montana, for example, requires each pet food to be registered by the manufacturer or the guarantor, and renewed each year ([MCA 80-9-201](https://mca.legmt.gov/bills/mca/title_0800/chapter_0090/part_0020/section_0010/0800-0090-0020-0010.html)).

Under either model, settle three points in writing: whose name goes on the label as guarantor, who files and pays for state registrations, and who keeps the guaranteed analysis and ingredient statement current if the formula changes.

## What does it cost to switch later?

Switching costs are where the two models differ most over time.

- **Private label:** if the recipe belongs to the manufacturer, leaving means reformulating. Your customers may notice the change, and you redo labels and registrations.
- **Co-packing:** if you own the formula and the specifications, you can move it to another plant or add a second source. Expect new samples and some adjustment, but the product stays yours.

## When private label fits

- You want to test demand before investing in a formula.
- You sell mainly through your own channel: a store, a groomer, a website, a subscription.
- Your brand story does not depend on a unique recipe.
- You need product on the shelf within weeks, not months.

## When co-packing fits

- Your formula is the reason customers buy.
- You plan to pitch retail buyers who will compare you to similar products.
- You want the option to change plants or add a second source.
- You already have a proven recipe from home or commissary production.

Many brands start with private label to prove demand and move to a custom formula once the numbers justify it. That path works if you plan for the relabel and re-registration it involves. For how to screen plants, see [how to find a dog treat co-packer](/guides/how-to-find-a-dog-treat-co-packer/).

## Questions to ask before you choose

1. Is this formula sold to other brands, and will you tell me which?
2. Can I change the formula, and who owns the changed version?
3. What are the minimums per SKU for stock and for custom formulas?
4. Whose name goes on the label as guarantor?
5. Who files state registrations and pays the fees?
6. If I leave, what can I take with me: formula, specifications, artwork?
7. What would it cost to move from your stock treat to a custom version later?
