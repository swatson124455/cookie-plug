## Why minimums exist

Every time a line switches products, the plant cleans it, re-sets it, and runs product until the first good unit comes off. That costs hours of labor and some ingredients whether you order 5,000 units or 50,000. The minimum is the order size where your run covers that cost.

It is also why minimums differ by product. A simple cookie usually changes over faster than a sandwich cookie with a cream filling, and a stock pouch changes over faster than a custom carton.

## Why minimums keep going up

A busy plant would rather run one large customer for three days than three small ones. Raising minimums is how a full plant says no to small brands without saying no.

The opposite is also true. A plant with open capacity gains more from filling a shift than from holding out for a bigger order, which gives it a reason to make a first run work and to grow the minimum with you.

## What you actually pay

Usually three kinds of cost:

| Cost | What it covers | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Per-unit price | Production, and often ingredients and packaging | Which ingredients and packaging are included? What is the price at two volumes? |
| One-time fees | Formulation, sampling, setup, printing plates | Is the formulation fee credited against the first order? |
| Freight | Getting pallets to your warehouse or retailer | Where does it ship from, and who books it? |

Some plants quote an all-in price: they buy the ingredients and packaging and sell you finished goods. Others charge a tolling fee to process ingredients you supply. All-in is simpler; tolling can be cheaper if you already buy ingredients well. Either way, ask for the price at two volumes so you can see the curve.

## Lead times, realistically

From approved sample to first production run: weeks at a plant with room, months at a full one. On repeat runs, from purchase order to ship, a few weeks is normal.

Packaging sets the pace as often as production does. Printed film and cartons can take weeks to arrive, so order them before you need them. Late packaging is one of the most common reasons a first run slips.

## Making a first run work

Arguing the minimum down rarely works. Making the first run cheap for the plant does.

- **Use stock or simple packaging** for the first run so the changeover is short.
- **Be flexible on timing** so the plant can slot you into an open shift.
- **Give a clean forecast** for the next three runs. A plant bends for a customer who looks like a good repeat.
- **Start with one or two SKUs.** Add flavors once the first one runs cleanly.

## The number that matters more than the minimum

Your reorder. A retailer's first order is a test; the second is the business. If your co-packer cannot run the reorder on time, the listing is at risk.

Ask every plant one question: "If my retailer doubles the order in month three, what happens?" The answer tells you whether it has capacity or a waitlist. If you already depend on one plant, read about [adding a second source](/guides/second-source-co-packer-strategy/).
