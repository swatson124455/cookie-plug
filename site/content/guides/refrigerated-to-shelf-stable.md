## Is your product refrigerated for safety or for quality?

Start with why it sits in the cold case today, because the answer sets the size of the project.

Some products are refrigerated for safety. The filling, topping, or crumb holds enough available water, at a high enough pH, to let harmful bacteria grow at room temperature. FDA's [Food Code](https://www.fda.gov/food/retail-food-protection/fda-food-code), the model that state and local regulators use for restaurants and grocery stores, decides whether a food needs [time and temperature control for safety](https://www.fda.gov/files/food/published/Job-Aid--Time-and-Temperature-Control-for-Safety-Foods.pdf) by its water activity and pH, alone or together, and the cutoffs differ depending on whether the food is heat-treated and packaged. Converting a product like this is a real reformulation, and it needs a lab's evaluation before it sells at room temperature.

Other products are refrigerated for quality: the cold slows mold and buys a few extra days. For these, the work is mostly about mold, texture, and packaging.

## What is water activity, and why does it decide shelf life?

Water activity (aw) measures how much of the water in a food is free to support microbial growth and chemical reactions. [FDA's inspection guide](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/inspection-technical-guides/water-activity-aw-foods) defines it as the ratio of the food's water vapor pressure to that of pure water under the same conditions, so it runs from 0 to 1. It is not the same as moisture content. Water bound up by sugar, salt, proteins, or starches is not free water ([Virginia Tech Extension](https://www.pubs.ext.vt.edu/FST/fst-485/fst-485.html)), so two cookies with the same moisture can have different water activity and different shelf lives.

The thresholds explain why mold is the usual limit for baked goods. According to FDA's guide, most bacteria do not grow below a water activity of 0.91, while most molds stop growing only below 0.80, and some specialized molds and yeasts have been reported growing at 0.65 and 0.60. Molds can grow on intermediate-moisture foods such as bread and other bakery products where bacteria cannot ([Journal of Food Protection](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0362028X23054819)).

Mold is not the only clock. Baked goods also go stale, lose or gain moisture, and oxidize ([IFT](https://www.ift.org/news-and-publications/food-technology-magazine/issues/2019/january/columns/packaging-solutions-for-baked-goods)). A mold inhibitor will not stop staling, so find out which failure ends your product's life first. Begin by having a lab measure the water activity of your current product; that number is your baseline.

## Which levers lower the risk?

Most conversions combine several levers, because each one costs something in taste, texture, label, or price.

| Lever | What it does | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Reformulation | Less water, or more ingredients that bind it | Texture and sweetness change |
| Humectants (sugar, salt, glycerin) | Bind water so less is free for mold | Taste, texture, and the label |
| Preservatives or clean-label inhibitors | Slow mold at a given water activity | New label ingredients; some retailers restrict them |
| Barrier packaging | Slows moisture and oxygen moving in or out | Film cost and new specs |
| Oxygen absorbers or nitrogen flush | Cut the oxygen left in the package | Need a high-barrier package to hold |

### Reformulation and humectants

Humectants lower water activity because water binds to them preferentially, and the common ones include salt, sugar, and glycerol (glycerin) ([AQUALAB](https://aqualab.com/en/knowledge-base/education-guides/food-manufacturers-step-step-guide-lowering-water-activity)). How much you add sets how far the number drops. Each step down also changes texture and sweetness, so work in rounds and taste every sample against the original.

### Preservatives and clean-label alternatives

The chemical preservatives most used against mold in commercial baking include propionates, sorbates, and benzoates. Clean-label alternatives include fermentation-derived ingredients such as cultured wheat, cultured dextrose, and vinegar-based ingredients ([Oklahoma State University Extension](https://extension.okstate.edu/fact-sheets/clean-label-mold-inhibitors-for-baking)). Treat either kind as one hurdle among several, not a fix for a product that is too moist.

Check your target retailers before you formulate. Whole Foods Market publishes [a list of ingredients it will not accept in food](https://www.wholefoodsmarket.com/quality-standards/food-ingredient-standards), and the list includes propionates, sorbates, and benzoates. A formula built around one of them is out at that retailer no matter how well it works.

### Packaging and oxygen control

Packaging decides whether your water activity target holds. A film that lets moisture through will let a product dry out or pick up moisture over its life. High-barrier films add an oxygen-barrier layer, commonly EVOH or PVDC. Oxygen absorbers and nitrogen flushing reduce the oxygen left in the package, which slows mold and rancidity, but they depend on a high-barrier package that keeps new oxygen out ([IFT](https://www.ift.org/news-and-publications/food-technology-magazine/issues/2019/january/columns/packaging-solutions-for-baked-goods)).

## How do you prove the shelf life?

There are two kinds of shelf-life test. Real-time testing stores the finished product, in its final package, under the conditions it will actually see, and checks it at set intervals. Accelerated testing stores it at a higher temperature on the assumption that problems will show up sooner ([Iowa State University](https://iastate.pressbooks.pub/foodproductdevelopment/chapter/shelf-life-and-abuse-testing/)).

For mold, the shortcut is a poor fit. [Campden BRI](https://www.campdenbri.co.uk/blogs/accelerated-shelf-life-testing.php) advises against using accelerated tests to set a microbiological shelf life, because changing the storage temperature can change which microorganisms grow and how fast. For a mold-limited product, plan on real-time data before you print a date.

At each pull, check mold, water activity, texture, flavor, and appearance. A food lab or an experienced process expert earns the fee here. They help set a water activity target for this formula in this package, decide whether you need a safety evaluation and not just a mold check, and read results that are easy to misjudge.

## What changes on the label?

More than the storage line.

- **Ingredient list.** New ingredients go in, in order of weight. A chemical preservative must be listed by name with a description of its function, such as "preservative," "to retard spoilage," or "a mold inhibitor" ([21 CFR 101.22](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-B/section-101.22)).
- **Allergens.** Check every new ingredient. A cultured wheat ingredient, for example, brings wheat, one of the [nine major food allergens](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/food-allergies), onto a label that may not have carried it.
- **Nutrition Facts.** A new formula means new numbers.
- **Storage statement.** "Keep refrigerated" comes off. If the product needs the refrigerator after opening, say that instead.
- **Date code.** Federal rules do not require a date on most foods (infant formula is the exception), and a "Best if Used By" date describes quality, not safety ([USDA FSIS](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/food-product-dating)). Set yours from real-time results, not from what a competitor prints.

## How do you bring it to a buyer?

As a new item, not a swap. A product that moves from the cold case to the shelf is shipped, stored, and received differently, and a substantial change in how a product is shipped, stored, or received is one of the three tests [GS1's GTIN rules](https://www.gs1.org/1/gtinrules/en/guiding-principles) use to decide whether a changed product needs a new GTIN. In practice, expect a new UPC, a new item setup, and a new place in the store, which may be a different category, with a different buyer, from your refrigerated item.

Bring the evidence: real-time shelf-life results, the finished spec, the new label, and pricing that reflects the new formula and package. Explain what the shelf-stable version can do that the refrigerated one cannot, such as ship without cold packs, sit in a different aisle, or go into a lunchbox. If you plan to keep selling the refrigerated version, say which stores get which item.

Check the retailer's ingredient standards before you start formulating, not after. The rest of the paperwork a new item needs is covered in [what big retailers require from a new supplier](/guides/what-retailers-require-from-a-new-supplier/).
