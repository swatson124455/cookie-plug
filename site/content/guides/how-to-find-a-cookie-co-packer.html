title: How to Find a Cookie Co-Packer (and What to Ask on the First Call) description: A practical guide for cookie brands outgrowing their kitchen: where co-packers are listed, how to qualify one, and the eight questions that decide whether they can make your product. date: 2026-10-01

Short answer: start with the co-manufacturer marketplaces (Keychain, PartnerSlate), the Specialty Food Association directory, and referrals from brokers and packaging suppliers; then qualify each candidate on line fit, minimums, lead time, certifications, and sampling speed before you spend a call on anything else. A plant with open capacity can sample in weeks; most others quote six to twelve months.

Where cookie co-packers are listed

There is no single directory, but four sources cover most of the US market. Keychain and PartnerSlate let you post a project and match against thousands of manufacturers. The Specialty Food Association and the trade press (Snack & Bakery, Commercial Baking) keep supplier directories. And the people who sell to bakeries every day, packaging reps, ingredient distributors, and food brokers, know which plants have room this quarter. Ask them first; it is faster than any list.

Qualify before you call

Most wasted calls come from a mismatch you could have caught on the website. Check three things: does the plant run your format (drop cookies, wire-cut, sandwich, bars, and soft-baked are different lines); is it shelf-stable or refrigerated; and does it say anything about minimums. If the site advertises "small batch" and your retailer needs 50,000 units a month, move on.

The eight questions for the first call

  1. What formats and pack sizes do you run today? The answer tells you whether your product needs new equipment.
  2. What is the minimum run per SKU, and is it flexible on a first run? Minimums are line economics, not policy; a plant with open capacity can bend.
  3. What is the lead time from purchase order to ship, on the first run and on repeats?
  4. Which certifications do you hold, and can you send the certificates? Ask for documents, not descriptions. Retail buyers will.
  5. How do you handle allergens? Shared lines with a cleaning protocol, or dedicated lines; the answer changes your label.
  6. How does sampling work, and how long does it take? Weeks is good. "After we finish the current backlog" is a no.
  7. Who owns the recipe if you develop it? Get this in writing before formulation starts.
  8. Who do I call when a run goes wrong? A named person, not a portal.

What to send after the call

Your product (a case, not a bag), the spec if you have one, your target retail price, and your realistic monthly volume for the next twelve months. A good co-packer benchmarks the product, sends it back with a spec sheet, and quotes from that. If they quote before tasting anything, be careful.

The most common mistake

Choosing on price per unit alone. The cost that hurts is the reorder you cannot fill because the plant is full. A slightly higher unit cost from a plant with capacity beats a lower one from a plant that puts you at the back of the line.
